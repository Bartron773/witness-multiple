## THE COMPARATIVE QUESTION

What becomes visible when these five independent arguments are placed beside one another?

Do not summarize five documents sequentially.

Do not produce a vote.

Do not determine a winner.

Do not force consensus.

Instead, investigate the relationships among the arguments.

The comparative field should distinguish at least the following:

### 1. Independent Convergence

Identify ideas that multiple witnesses reached independently before seeing one another's contributions.

Ask:

- What genuinely similar conclusions appeared?
- Did witnesses arrive at similar conclusions through different reasoning?
- Are apparently similar statements actually making different claims?
- Does convergence strengthen an idea, complicate it, or merely show that several witnesses encountered the same conceptual pressure?

Do not treat convergence as proof.

---

### 2. Genuine Divergence

Identify places where the witnesses materially differ.

Preserve those differences.

Ask:

- Where do the arguments assign authority differently?
- Where does one witness permit something another distrusts?
- Where do ethical, archival, technical, psychological, relational, or spatial perspectives produce different consequences?
- Are any disagreements irreducible without privileging one value over another?

Do not repair disagreement merely to produce a cleaner synthesis.

---

### 3. Unique Contributions

Identify concepts, distinctions, metaphors, questions, or argumentative moves substantially foregrounded by only one witness.

A unique contribution should not be treated as weaker merely because it lacks convergence.

Ask what became visible because this particular witness was present.

---

### 4. Unresolved Collisions

Identify places where legitimate claims remain in tension even after comparison.

These may include collisions between:

- subject and witness;
- consent and evidence;
- privacy and historical integrity;
- self-definition and shared history;
- archival responsibility and human sovereignty;
- standing to speak and authority to decide;
- individual change and consequences for another person;
- competing stakeholders in the same memory.

Do not force these collisions into solutions.

---

### 5. Interpretive Uncertainty

Mark places where the comparison itself is uncertain.

If two witnesses appear to agree but may be using different concepts, say so.

If a disagreement depends upon interpretation, say so.

If one contribution cannot be cleanly categorized, preserve that ambiguity.

The comparative layer is itself an interpretation and should not pretend otherwise.

---

## PROVENANCE OF COMPARISON

Your comparative account is a new witness act.

You are no longer only examining the original Commission 06 question.

You are interpreting five other interpretations of it.

Therefore distinguish between:

- what a witness explicitly argued;
- what you infer from that argument;
- relationships you identify between witnesses;
- conclusions you yourself introduce through comparison.

Do not attribute your synthesis to the witnesses.

Do not write:

> "The five witnesses established..."

when the evidence supports only:

> "Placed beside one another, several accounts appear to..."

The arranger leaves fingerprints.

Your comparison should leave yours visible.

---

## QUESTIONS WORTH TESTING

You may investigate questions such as:

Does standing belong to a person, or does it attach to particular operations performed upon a memory?

Can multiple parties possess legitimate but non-equivalent standing over the same record?

Does standing grant a right to speak, a right to contest, a right to restrict use, a right to withdraw, or actual authority to decide?

Can someone have standing without having final authority?

Does the archive itself possess standing, or only responsibility?

Does a model possess standing because it witnessed something, or merely provenance-bearing responsibility for what it represented?

When a memory implicates several lives, can any single party legitimately control all dimensions of that memory?

What happens when protecting one person's sovereignty necessarily limits another person's legitimate claim upon the same history?

These are prompts for investigation, not required conclusions.

New questions are welcome.

Contradictions are welcome.

Do not force resolution.
